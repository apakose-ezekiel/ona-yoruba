01 — Overview

What is Kójódá?

Kójódá is the pre-colonial Yoruba calendar system — a complete framework for measuring time rooted in Yoruba cosmology, agricultural cycles, and Òrìṣà worship. The name comes from the phrase Kí Ọjọ́ Dá: "May the day be clearly foreseen." The calendar does not merely count days. It assigns spiritual significance to every day through the four-day Òrìṣà cycle, anchoring time to devotion rather than administration.

Today, the Kójódá is actively maintained and published monthly by Yoruba traditionalists. The Dundun Centre, among others, publicly advocates for its recognition as a living cultural system — not an archaeological relic. As of June 6, 2026, the Ọdún Ifá Àgbáyé (World Ifá Festival) was observed in Ilé-Ifẹ̀, with pilgrims journeying to the holy hills of Òkè Ìtasẹ̀ and Òkè Ìgẹ̀tí.

Source Conflict — Weeks per Month

The infographic states 4 weeks per month. The Dundun Centre press release states 7 weeks per month. Both arrive at 28-day months and 364-day years through the same math (4 × 7 = 28). The practical Kójódá monthly listing contains 30 entries — which fits neither cleanly. This calendar renders the mathematically clean 28-day / 7-week version, consistent with the 364-day annual total. Requires verification from a primary Babaláwo source.

04 — The Four-Day Week

Ọsẹ — The Sacred Week Cycle

Where the Gregorian week names Roman and Norse gods (Saturn → Saturday, Thor → Thursday), the Yoruba week names living Òrìṣà — deities still actively worshipped. Every day is a devotional appointment. The four days cycle without interruption across months and the full year.

05 — Daily Time System

Àkókò Ọjọ́ — Naming the Hours

Before clocks, Yoruba divided the day into 11 named periods — each describing the quality of light, heat, and activity rather than arbitrary 60-minute blocks. Unlike the Gregorian clock's uniform hours, these periods vary in length. Time was phenomenological: defined by what was happening, not by mechanical division.

06 — Year Count & Historical Claims

10,000+ Years — What the Claim Means

The Kójódá calendar counts Yoruba civilisation at over 10,000 years old. As of June 3, 2026 CE, Yoruba Year 10,068 began. The Dundun Centre press release and the Kójódá practical listing for April 2026 give slightly different figures — 10,068 and 10,067 respectively — but this reflects a boundary moment: 10,067 was the year that ended June 2, and 10,068 opened June 3. Not a contradiction.

[Inference] — Historical Claim Context

The infographic states 1958 = Yoruba Year 10,000, placing Year 1 at approximately 8,042 BCE. Current peer-reviewed archaeology does not date complex Yoruba cultural settlement to 8,042 BCE, though Ile-Ife's sacred geography and oral tradition encoded in Ifá place the origin of humanity there within Yoruba cosmology. This is civilizational dating rooted in cosmological tradition — the same category as Hebrew dating from Genesis or Chinese dating from the Yellow Emperor. It does not need to match Western archaeological consensus to be valid within its own epistemic framework.

07 — Global Context

Kójódá Among the World's Calendars

Every major civilisation counts time from its own origin point. The Gregorian calendar's dominance is political and commercial — not evidence that other systems are less sophisticated. The Kójódá shares structural features with calendars from cultures that have never been in contact with Yoruba civilisation: the Ethiopian calendar also runs 13 months; the Coptic also has a small remainder month; the Hebrew also anchors to a cosmological creation moment.
